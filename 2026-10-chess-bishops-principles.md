- **Source**: *Winning Chess Strategies* by Yasser Seirawan (with Jeremy Silman) — Chapter Four, "Understanding Where the Pieces Go," the Bishop section (pp. 58–63, Diagrams 32–39); pages photographed 2026-10-05.
- **One-liner**: A Bishop is **long-range but locked to one color and blockable by pawns**, so it is classified **good / bad / active** — and the crucial correction is that **good-vs-bad is a label about your pawns, while active-vs-inactive is the thing that actually decides the game**. A "bad" Bishop outside its pawn chain beats a "good" Bishop with nowhere to go.
- ## 1. The Bishop's two faces
	- **The upside**: it reaches **the other end of the board** in one move — long range, enormous reach on an open diagonal.
	- **The downside**: it is **easily blocked by pawns** and is **forever stuck in a one-colored dimension**.
	- Seirawan's point is that **neither attribute makes a Bishop strong or weak by itself** — *"it's up to the player to turn his Bishops into something special,"* which is what makes chess challenging.
	- **There are three types of Bishops: good, bad, and active.** Understanding the classification matters for one compelling reason: *if you don't, you won't have any control over how your Bishops turn out.*
- ## 2. The three classifications
	- | Type | Definition | Why it matters |
	  |---|---|---|
	  | **Good** | Its **central pawns are *not* on its color**, so they are not obstructing its activity | Diagram 32: White's light-squared Bishop eyes **two** wonderful diagonals (h1–a8, a2–g8) because no White pawn blocks its path. With the Rook it wins material: **1.Re7** double-attacks f7 and b7 |
	  | **Bad** | Its **central pawns *are* on its color**, blocking its activity | Diagram 33: White is **a whole Bishop up** and the game is still **a draw** — the *lone* Bishop (no other pieces to work with) can do nothing and the King can't break in |
	  | **Active** | Either good or bad — **called active simply because it serves an active function** | Diagram 34: Black's Bishop is good *by definition* and White's is bad, **yet White is winning** (1.Ra8 Rf8 2.Rb8 and 3.Bxb7) because White's bad Bishop sits **outside** the pawn chain and Black's good Bishop has **hardly any squares to go to** |
	- **The two strategic maxims stated in the text:**
		- ***Place a Bishop on an unobstructed diagonal; the rewards will pour in immediately.***
		- ***If your Bishop is on the same color as your pawns, you will severely limit its activity.***
	- **The correction that matters most**: ***A bad Bishop can be a strong piece — if you can get it to an active post.*** Good and bad are descriptions of your pawn structure. **Active is a description of the piece's actual life.** Judge by the second.
	- **The one-color limitation, in its purest form**: in Diagram 33, even **adding a second White Bishop on h3** would not win, because all of Black's pawns sit on dark squares. Two Bishops would be *"nothing more than ghosts, floating around the board but unable to touch anything."* Material is irrelevant if it cannot touch the enemy.
- ## 3. The decision procedure
	- **How to judge a Bishop, in order:**
	  ```
	    Are your own central pawns on your Bishop's color?
	            |                                  |
	           NO                                 YES
	            |                                  |
	      "GOOD" Bishop                      "BAD" Bishop
	            |                                  |
	            +-------- but ask the REAL question --------+
	                                |
	                 Does it have an open diagonal to work on?
	                      |                        |
	                     YES                      NO  -> don't panic; do one of:
	                      |                             1. free its diagonals
	                 ACTIVE = strong                       (move pawns off its color)
	                                                    2. get it OUTSIDE the pawn chain
	                                                    3. trade it off
	  ```
- ## 4. The three cures for a bad Bishop
	- ### Cure 1 — free the Bishop's diagonals
		- **Move your pawns to another color** so the diagonals open. Diagram 35: White's Bishop looks bad because the center pawns on **d4 and f4** block it on dark squares — but *"the Bishop's badness is an illusion,"* because those pawns can easily move.
		- The method: **1.f5** (freeing the c1–h6 diagonal), then **2.d5** (freeing the e1–a7 diagonal), and after **3.Nxe5 dxe5 4.Bxb6** White wins — the capture on b6 also picks up the a5-pawn.
		- **The lesson**: before writing a Bishop off, check whether the pawns blocking it are **mobile**. A bad Bishop behind movable pawns is a temporary condition, not a verdict.
	- ### Cure 2 — get the Bishop outside the pawn chain
		- A Bishop stuck *behind* its own pawn chain is inactive; the fix is to **walk it around to the outside**. Diagram 36: White's Bishop on d2 is bad and inactive — but it doesn't have to stay. **Bc1 then Ba3** brings it to life on the a3–f8 diagonal; **Be1 then Bh4** gives it the h4–d8 diagonal.
		- ***Bishops are strongest in wide-open positions. A bad Bishop can only become active if you get it outside the pawn chain.***
		- **The corollary for the whole game**: because Bishops can't jump over anything, **if you have Bishops you must always be trying to rip open the center.** A closed, locked pawn formation is nothing but a hindrance to them.
			- Compare the two openings the book sets side by side: after **1.e4 e5 2.d4 exd4 3.c3 dxc3 4.Bc4 cxb2 5.Bxb2** the Bishops rule a wide-open board and Knights can't compete (White has paid **two pawns** for it). After **1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 c5 3.d5 e5 4.Nc3 d6 5.e4**, with the center **completely locked up**, those same Bishops are not nearly so valuable.
		- **The applied example** (Diagram 37): Black has a dark-squared Bishop and White does not. *Is this important? Only if you want it to be!* Since White's center pawns block it, Black plays **1...c5!** to blast open the h8–a1 diagonal; after **2.dxc5 Nxc5**, the very piece that was blocked a moment ago **is now shredding the board**.
	- ### Cure 3 — when all else fails, trade
		- If a bad Bishop **cannot be activated by either method above, trade it for a piece of equal or greater value.**
		- Diagram 38: White is more comfortable — two Bishops, a Knight with access to b5 and d5, and Black's lone Bishop is bad, blocked by the e5-pawn. Black's plan is to **swap the poor g7-Bishop for White's very active e3-Bishop**:
			- **1...h5!** — preparing the swap.
			- **2.Nb5 Rc8 3.c3** — good play: the c3-pawn keeps Black out of **d4 and b4**. *"Notice how masters always try to deprive enemy pieces of good squares."*
			- **3...Kh7** — the point of 1...h5; Black is ready for ...Bh6.
			- **4.Na3 Bh6 5.Bxh6 Qxh6 6.Qxh6+ Kxh6** — Black has **equalized** and eventually drew.
		- ***If you don't like the piece you own, improve its position or trade it.***
- ## 5. Closed centers push the play to the wings
	- **Larsen–Fischer, Candidates Match 1971** is given as a game where *"no less an authority than Bobby Fischer uses the laws we have been discussing."*
	- The structural point (Diagram 39): **the center is closed, so both sides are busy with pawn breaks on the wings** — White making considerable queenside progress. A locked center doesn't just dull the Bishops; **it relocates the entire game to where pawns can still be levered open.**
- ## 6. Takeaways
	- **"Good" and "bad" describe your pawns. "Active" describes your Bishop.** The classification is a diagnostic, not a verdict — and the book's own examples (Diagram 34 especially) show the technically bad Bishop beating the technically good one.
	- **The question to ask every time is "does it have a diagonal?"** Not "are my pawns on its color?" The pawn-color test is just the fastest way to predict the answer.
	- **A Bishop's problem is almost always fixable, and there are exactly three fixes**: move the blocking pawns, walk the Bishop outside the chain, or trade it. Diagnose first, then pick one — *don't panic*.
	- **If you own Bishops, open the position; if you own Knights, close it.** Bishops want ripped-open centers; a locked structure is pure hindrance to them — which is the exact mirror of the Knight's preference in [[2026-10-chess-knights-principles]]. Piece value is contextual, and **you have a say in the context.**
	- **Material you cannot touch is not material.** The two-ghost-Bishops observation in Diagram 33 is the cleanest statement of that in the chapter: an extra piece that can never reach an enemy man is worth nothing.
	- **Deprive enemy pieces of good squares.** White's 3.c3 in Diagram 38 is a small move doing strategic work — the defensive twin of the Knight's "support point" idea.
