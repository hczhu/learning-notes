- **Source**: *Winning Chess Strategies* by Yasser Seirawan (with Jeremy Silman) — Chapter Four, "Understanding Where the Pieces Go," the Rook section (pp. 66–70, Diagrams 40–43, Tests 6 and 8); pages photographed 2026-10-05.
- **One-liner**: Rooks are the most neglected pieces on the board, and the entire remedy is one pipeline: **open file → 7th or 8th rank**. The file is never the goal — it is the **road** to the rank where the enemy pawns live and the enemy King is trapped. Double on the file to win it; double on the 7th and you usually just win.
- ## 1. Why Rooks get ignored
	- **History**: before the Queen's modern move was introduced in the **15th century**, the Rook was **by far the strongest piece in the game** — so powerful that a player attacking it was expected to call **"check-Rook."**
	- **The Rook's own powers never changed**; only its *status* was diminished by the arrival of the modern Queen. It remains *"a piece of extraordinary importance."*
	- **Who overlooks it**: *"most amateurs, who simply don't know how to make use of a Rook's considerable strength."* Beginners ignore their Rooks at the start of a game, and **even seasoned tournament players of class A or expert strength fail to make the most of them.** This is not purely a novice error.
	- **The structural reason, which is worth internalizing**: Rooks **sit at the sides of the board and are usually the last pieces developed.** The typical player pushes a few pawns ("the humble foot soldiers"), develops Bishops and Knights ("the warriors on horseback"), brings up the Queen ("the all-powerful general"), and **launches a premature attack** — without the backup of the Rooks, *the cannons*.
	- Diagram 40 is the cannon effect in one move: **1.Qg1** and there is **no answer to the threatened 2.Rg8 checkmate**. The rout is possible only because the Rook was already positioned to fire.
- ## 2. The rule, and the thing the rule is *for*
	- ***To be effective, Rooks need to be placed on open files.***
	- But the open file is the **means**, not the end. Nimzovich, quoted in the chapter:
		- > *"The aim of all maneuvers along this file is the ultimate intrusion along this file onto the 7th or 8th rank, i.e., into the enemy position."*
	- **Why the 7th rank specifically**, in the book's own two reasons:
		- **Most enemy pawns reside there** — a Rook on the 7th eats them.
		- **Control of that critical rank tends to doom the enemy King.**
	- **The pipeline:**
	  ```
	    8 |  . . . . . . . .   <- 8th rank: back-rank mate lives here
	    7 |  p p p . . p p p   <- 7th rank: the enemy pawns live here   *** GOAL ***
	    6 |  . . . . . . . .
	    5 |  . . . . . . . .
	      |        ^
	      |        |   the OPEN FILE is only the ROAD.
	      |        |   Winning the file is a means; arriving is the point.
	    1 |  . . . R . . . .   <- your Rook starts here
	  ```
	- Diagram 41 shows how little else is needed: **material is even**, but **1.Rc7** wins easily. The Black King is **trapped on the back rank**, **2.Rxa7** is threatened, and the natural **1...Ra8 is met by 2.Rd7**, picking up the d-pawn. A Rook on the 7th attacks in two directions at once and the defender runs out of hands.
- ## 3. Doubling — how you actually win a file
	- **Doubling** = *"you attempt to lay claim to a particular file with two Rooks, or a Rook and Queen."* It is how a contested file becomes **yours**.
	- **Tripling is stronger still.** In Diagram 43 the c-file is the only open file on the board, and **Alekhine brings all his big guns to bear on it** — the chapter calls it a demonstration of *how a file should be taken*.
	- **Doubled Rooks on the 7th rank are called "Pigs on the 7th"** — *"extremely strong and, in most cases, constitute a winning advantage."* In that game: **9.Kd2 Nb6 10.R2e7** (note the move needs disambiguating — the Rooks are stacked), **10...Nd5 11.Rf7+ Ke8 12.Rb7 Nxb4 13.Bc4** and **Black resigns**, since 13...Nc6 14.Rh7 Rf6 15.Rh8+ Rf8 16.Bf7+ is immediate death.
	- **The closing strategy of the whole section**:
		- > ***Whenever possible, get your Rooks to open files that will allow them to move to the 7th or 8th rank. And if you can double your Rooks on the 7th rank, you'll usually have an enormous advantage.***
- ## 4. The model game — Botvinnik–Alekhine, A.V.R.O. 1938
	- White is already better in Diagram 42 **because both Rooks are on the open c- and e-files**. The whole technique is a sequence of small, nameable decisions worth stealing:
	- | Move | What it does | The transferable idea |
	  |---|---|---|
	  | **1.Qc2** | Doubles on the c-file with Queen and Rook | *"The c-file is mine!"* — **claim the file before you use it** |
	  | **1...Re7** | Black can no longer contest c with ...Rc7, so he fights for the e-file instead | **Losing one file means you must not lose the other too** |
	  | **2.Rxe7 Qxe7 3.Qc7!** | Trades Queens so the Rook can penetrate | **Trade pieces to clear the path to the 7th** — the Queen trade is the price of entry |
	  | **3...Qxc7 4.Rxc7 f6 5.Kf1** | The King steps up | **One move, three jobs**: enters the battle, prevents future **back-rank mates**, denies e2 to the enemy Rook |
	  | **5...Rf7 6.Rc8+ Rf8 7.Rc3!** | Declines the trade and retreats | ***Don't trade any of your good pieces for the enemy's inferior ones!*** |
	  | **7...g5 8.Ne1 h5 9.h4!** | Provokes | **Force a concession**: 9...g4 hands White f4 after Nd3–f4; 9...gxh4 10.Nf3 regains the pawn and leaves **a shattered pawn formation** |
	  | **9...Nd7 10.Rc7 Rf7 11.Nf3 g4 12.Ne1** | Spends time deliberately | **White was willing to waste a little time to force Black to create a hole on f4** — then routes the Knight to it |
	  | **13.Nd3 f4 14.f3 gxf3 15.gxf3 a5 16.a4!** | Fixes the b-pawn on b6 | **Fix enemy pawns on squares where you can attack them** |
	  | **16...Kf8 17.Rc6** | The payoff | *"The difference in Rooks is striking"* — White's **eyes all the Black pawns** while Black's is **purely defensive** |
	- The finish is the Rook simply **eating the position**: 17...Ke7 18.Kf2 Rf5 19.b3 Kd8 20.Ke2 Nb8 21.Rg6 Kc7 22.Ne5 Na6 23.Rg7+ Kc8 24.Nc6 Rf6 25.Ne7+ Kb8 26.Nxd5 Rd6 27.Rg5 Nb4 28.Nxb4 axb4 29.Rxh5 Rc6 30.Rb5 Kc7 31.Rxb4 Rh6 32.Rb5 Rxh4 33.Kd3, and White wins.
	- **The pattern across the whole game**: Botvinnik never wins material by tactics. He wins the **file**, trades into the **rank**, and then the material falls off the board on its own.
- ## 5. Active vs. passive Rooks
	- The sharpest single judgment in the section is the comparison at move 17: **one Rook eyeing every enemy pawn, the other tied to defending them.** Same piece, same material count, completely different game.
	- The diagnostic to carry: **ask what your Rook is doing, not where it is.** A Rook that is *guarding* is a Rook the opponent has effectively captured.
	- Note how the activity is *created* — not found. Botvinnik provokes `...g5/...h5` weaknesses (9.h4), fixes the b-pawn (16.a4), and only then does 17.Rc6 have targets. **A Rook needs objects to attack, and you manufacture those first.**
- ## 6. Takeaways
	- **Open file → 7th/8th rank is the whole plan.** Everything else — doubling, trading Queens, the King step-up — is logistics in service of that one route. If you only remember one thing about Rooks, remember the destination, not the file.
	- **Develop your Rooks before you attack.** The chapter's diagnosis of the typical game is that the attack starts *"prematurely"* precisely because the cannons were left at home. Diagram 40 is what the same attack looks like with them.
	- **Doubling is how you win a contested file; tripling wins it outright.** Claiming a file is an actual operation that costs moves, not something that happens for free.
	- **"Pigs on the 7th" usually just win.** Two Rooks on the 7th is close to a winning condition by itself — worth treating as a target in its own right.
	- **Don't trade a good piece for a bad one.** Botvinnik's 7.Rc3! is a whole technique in one move: once your Rook is better than theirs, **avoiding the trade is the win**. (The mirror of the Bishop advice in [[2026-10-chess-bishops-principles]], where you trade *away* a piece you don't like.)
	- **Manufacture the weaknesses before you invade.** Provoke a pawn move, fix the pawn on a reachable square, *then* bring the Rook. The Knight's support point is created the same way in [[2026-10-chess-knights-principles]] — across all three pieces, the lesson is that good squares and good targets are **built, not discovered.**
