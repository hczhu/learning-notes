- **Source**: Bent Flyvbjerg and Dan Gardner, *How Big Things Get Done: The Surprising Factors That Determine the Fate of Every Project, from Home Renovations to Space Exploration and Everything In Between* (Currency, 2023).
- **One-liner**: **Think slow, act fast.** Do the slow, cheap thinking (planning) up front, so the expensive, risky part (delivery) can run quickly before things go wrong.
- ## Key Idea: Think Slow, Act Fast
	- The book's answer to how a vision turns into a plan that becomes a "triumphant new reality" (Introduction, p. xvi).
	- **Model project: the Empire State Building**, officially opened by President Hoover on May 1, 1931, *exactly as scheduled*.
		- **Fast delivery**: at peak the crew erected 14½ floors in 10 working days, "steel, concrete, stone and all" (partner Richmond Shreve).
		- **Construction as a vertical assembly line**: the designers modeled the process on car factories, except "the assembly line did the moving" while "the finished product stayed in place."
		- **Under budget**: estimated at \$50 million, it actually cost \$41 million (\$679 million in 2021 dollars). That's 17% under budget, or \$141 million in 2021 dollars. Construction finished several weeks before the opening ceremony.
		- **Efficiency and beauty together**: architect William Lamb's drive for practicality produced a lean, elegant design that won the New York chapter of the AIA's 1931 Medal of Honor.
- ## Project Risk Is Fat-Tailed (Ch. 1, pp. 10–11)
	- **Thin vs. fat tails**
		- **Normal (thin-tailed)**: height. Most adult males are about 5'9" (1.75 m), and the tallest person in the world is only about **1.6×** taller.
		- **Fat-tailed**: far more extreme outcomes in the tails. Wealth: the richest person is **3,134,707×** wealthier than the average person. If height were distributed like wealth, the tallest person would be **3,311 miles (5,329 km)** tall, with their head 13× farther into space than the International Space Station.
	- **The critical question**: are project outcomes distributed normally, or do they have fat tails?
	- **Answer: most project types have fat tails.** IT projects in Flyvbjerg's database:
		- **18%** have cost overruns above **50%** in real terms.
		- Within that tail, the *average* overrun is **447%**, so many have even higher overruns.
	- **Fat-tailed project types, from fattest to least fat (but still fat)** (data in Appendix A):
		- IT → nuclear storage → Olympic Games → nuclear power plants → big hydroelectric dams → airports → defense → big buildings → aerospace → tunnels → mining → high-speed rail → urban rail → conventional rail → bridges → oil → gas → water.
		- **A few project types do *not* have fat tails.** The book explains why, and how to use that fact, in the last chapter.
	- **The lesson**: "simple, clear, and scary." Most big projects aren't just at risk of not delivering as promised, or of going seriously wrong. They're at risk of going **disastrously** wrong, because their risk is fat-tailed.
		- Yet the project management literature almost completely ignores systematic study of fat-tailed project risk.
	- **What fat-tailed outcomes look like**
		- | Project | Outcome |
		  | --- | --- |
		  | Boston's "Big Dig" (elevated highway replaced with a tunnel; started 1991) | 16 years of disruption; cost more than **triple** the plan |
		  | NASA's James Webb Space Telescope | Forecast 12 years, took 19; final cost \$8.8 billion, **450%** over budget |
		  | Canada's firearms registry (IT project) | **590%** over budget |
		  | Scotland's Parliament Building | Opened in 2004, 3 years late and **978%** over budget |
	- **Black swans**: Nassim Nicholas Taleb's term for low-probability, high-consequence events. Disastrous project outcomes qualify; they can end careers and sink companies.
		- **Kmart**: launched two enormous IT projects in 2000 in response to Walmart and Target. Costs exploded, contributing directly to its bankruptcy filing in 2002.
