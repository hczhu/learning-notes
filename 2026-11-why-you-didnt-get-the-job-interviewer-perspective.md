- **Source**: "Why you didn't get the job, even though the interview went well (interviewer perspective)", an anonymous post by someone who regularly sits on hiring panels. The user pasted the text on 2026-11-15; the author, venue, and date weren't provided.
- **One-liner**: A rejection after a good-feeling interview usually isn't proof that you messed up, or that the process was fake. Hiring is a **relative, multi-person decision against partly hidden criteria**, and most of the factors that decide it are outside the candidate's control.

- ## Six reasons a "good" interview still ends in rejection
	- | # | Reason | What happens | In your control? |
	  |---|---|---|---|
	  | 1 | **Weak answer on a core requirement** | You were brilliant on nice-to-haves but weaker on one mandatory skill, and the mandatory skill decides the outcome. Postings often don't make clear which requirements really matter. | **Partly**: ask which requirements matter most. |
	  | 2 | **Someone else was already the favorite** | A more influential panelist preferred someone else, often a former colleague. Or a strong internal candidate only had to avoid falling flat. | No |
	  | 3 | **Great person, wrong role** | You came across as too junior, too senior, or skewed toward a different specialty. Example: a fascinating, very experienced candidate who mostly oversaw work, when the role needed a hands-on **doer**. | Mostly no |
	  | 4 | **Hidden requirements you didn't meet** | Unwritten preferences never make the posting, and some postings are simply poorly written. Examples below. | No (and usually invisible) |
	  | 5 | **Someone else was slightly better** | With many strong candidates, the tiebreaker is small and subjective, such as who covered more of the bonus skills. | Barely |
	  | 6 | **It didn't actually go well** | The panel was being polite. Some interviewers are *extra* warm to a candidate who has no chance, out of sympathy, with follow-up questions, generous prompting, and validation. | **Yes**: get honest outside feedback. |

- ## Details worth remembering
	- ### Hidden requirements (reason 4)
		- This happens **more than candidates realize**. Two examples from the author's panels:
			- **No room for advancement**: they rejected a clearly ambitious candidate because they wanted someone who would be content in a dead-end role for longer.
			- **A difficult stakeholder**: they rejected a candidate who seemed too deferential and agreeable, because the role needed someone self-assured enough to stand their ground with a strong, difficult personality.
		- Implication: even if you clearly met every *written* requirement, there may have been unwritten ones you didn't meet.
	- ### Warmth is not a signal (reason 6)
		- An interviewer's friendliness, follow-up questions, or building on your answers **don't reliably mean you're doing well**. They can be sympathy for a candidate who is struggling.
		- The author deliberately avoids this, to avoid leading candidates on, but other interviewers don't.
	- ### Panel dynamics (reason 2)
		- Your strongest advocate on the panel may not hold the deciding vote. An interviewer who is comfortable with a known quantity, such as a former colleague or an internal candidate, can outweigh the panel's enthusiasm for an outsider.

- ## What to do about it
	- **Ask what matters most, early in the interview.** This is especially useful when the posting lists many requirements. Suggested script:
		- *"I have wide-ranging experience, but for this interview I'd love to focus on what you're most interested in. From your list of job requirements, are there any you'd consider especially high priority that I should make sure to highlight?"*
		- This addresses reason 1 directly and may bring some hidden requirements (reason 4) to the surface.
	- **Make sure your answers on the must-have skills are strong.** Don't let brilliance on the bonus skills substitute for the core requirements.
	- **Get honest feedback through mock interviews** with people you know, to check whether you're reading the room correctly (reason 6).
	- **Don't over-learn from a single rejection.** For reasons 2–5 you may have done nothing wrong. Avoid both extremes: "I must have done something wrong" and "the whole system is fake."

- ## Takeaways
	- From the candidate's side, an interview feeling good is weak evidence about the outcome. The decision depends on **comparison with other candidates, who has the most influence on the panel, and unwritten fit criteria**.
	- The two levers you control: **find out which requirements are core and hit them hard**, and **calibrate how you come across** with honest outside feedback.
