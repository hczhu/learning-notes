- **Source**: *Winning Chess Strategies* by Yasser Seirawan (with Jeremy Silman) — Chapter Eight, “Territorial Domination,” pp. 159–175, from the supplied photographs.
- **One-liner**: Use pawns to restrict the opponent and give your pieces room; secure that territory, improve your pieces patiently, and advance only when the resulting position is better.

- ## Space Is Useful Room, Not Just Advanced Pawns

	- Space is the safe territory available for your pieces to maneuver, generally behind your pawn frontier. Squares the opponent controls are not automatically yours just because they lie behind your pawns.
	- More space lets you switch attacks and find good posts while enemy pieces obstruct one another. But every pawn advance creates a frontier you must defend and gives up control of some squares.

- ## Choose Your Plan According to Who Has More Space

	- | Decision | You have more space | You are cramped |
	  | --- | --- | --- |
	  | Piece exchanges | Usually avoid unnecessary trades: keep the opponent crowded. | Seek useful trades to free maneuvering room. |
	  | Piece placement | Improve your least useful piece; occupy strong posts before forcing action. | Coordinate pieces against the opponent's pawn center and its defenders. |
	  | Pawn play | Secure your center and restrain freeing pawn breaks before expanding. | Prepare a pawn break—a push that challenges or exchanges the restricting pawns—before the bind becomes permanent. |
	  | Timing | Squeeze patiently while denying counterplay. | Contest territory early; do not drift into passive defense. |
	- These are guidelines, not absolute rules. Exchange pieces or surrender space when it produces a better ending, lasting weaknesses to attack, or another concrete advantage.

- ## Handle the Center Carefully

	- **Maintain a strong center before pushing it.** Pawns on d4 and e4 deny enemy pieces c5, d5, e5, and f5. Support the pawns and improve your pieces; the restriction itself can be valuable.
	- **Check what an advance gives away.** Moving e4–e5 stops controlling d5 and f5; moving d4–d5 stops controlling c5 and e5. A bigger-looking center can free enemy pieces or block your own.
	- **When the center is locked, look to the wings.** Use your maneuvering room to prepare flank expansion, while checking whether the opponent can reopen the center against your king.
	- **Against a broad center, attack its base and defenders.** Concentrate pressure with pieces and pawn breaks. In the chapter's Grünfeld example, Black combines pressure on d4 with moves such as …Bg7, …c5, and …Nc6; the center becomes a target rather than an uncontested asset.

- ## Examples to Remember

	- **Silman–Formanek, 1989:** with the center locked, White prepares kingside expansion, avoids exchanges that would relieve Black, improves pieces, then gains queenside space too. **Lesson:** expand behind a secure center rather than rush a breakthrough.
	- **Capablanca–Lasker, 1921:** Black's freeing moves reduce White's space advantage but leave weak pawns. White switches to attacking those lasting targets. **Lesson:** convert space into another advantage instead of trying to preserve it forever.
	- **Lobron–Seirawan, 1982:** Black challenges White's central superiority with …c5 and …e5 while retaining queenside territory. **Lesson:** fight for space before the opponent completely restricts you.
	- **Yermolinsky–Seirawan, 1992:** Black pressures d4 and its defenders; White's imposing center eventually leaves a weak e5-pawn. **Lesson:** attack the pawn center, but check king safety before opening lines.

- ## Before Every Pawn Push or Exchange

	- Which squares will I gain—and stop controlling?
	- Will my pieces become more active, or merely my pawns more advanced?
	- What freeing break does my opponent want, and can I restrain it?
	- Does this trade relieve their cramped position, or give me something better?
	- Is their threat genuinely forcing? Calculate it, but do not abandon a sound central plan merely to answer a harmless threat.
