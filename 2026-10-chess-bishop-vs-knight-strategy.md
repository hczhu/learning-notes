- **Source**: *Winning Chess Strategies* by Yasser Seirawan (with Jeremy Silman) — Chapter Five, “Superior Minor Pieces,” photographed excerpts from pp. 90–91, 96–99, and 101. Some game continuations and Diagram 62 are outside the supplied excerpts.
- **One-liner**: Shape the position to suit your minor piece: open diagonals and deny outposts with a bishop; restrict the bishop and establish useful, secure posts with a knight.

- ## Choose a Plan from the Position
	- Both pieces are conventionally worth about three pawns, but their actual value depends on mobility, targets, and pawn structure. Neither is always superior.
	- | Strategic question | With the bishop | With the knight |
	  | --- | --- | --- |
	  | What pawn structure helps? | Open the position; advance or exchange pawns obstructing useful diagonals. | Keep useful central pawn chains locked to restrict the bishop. |
	  | Which squares matter? | Deny the enemy knight advanced support points, especially with pawn control. | Create an advanced outpost: a useful square supported by your pieces or pawns that enemy pawns cannot readily drive you from. |
	  | How should the piece work? | Use long diagonals to attack reachable targets and restrict the knight. | Plan a route to the outpost; use it to attack weaknesses, blockade pawns, or restrict the king. |
	  | What can undermine the plan? | An open diagonal is of little value if there are no relevant targets on its color. | A closed position alone is insufficient if the knight has no useful route or job. |

- ## Small Pawn Changes Can Reverse the Advantage
	- **Diagrams 55–56**: initially Black's bishop on e5 commands the h8–a1 diagonal, while White's knight has no advanced post.
	- Moving Black's bishop to g7 and the e6-pawn to e5 reverses the assessment: the pawn blocks the bishop and stops controlling d5. White can route the knight `Nb4–Nd5` to exploit the new outpost.
	- **Action**: before a pawn move, check both the diagonal it opens or closes and the squares it stops defending. A pawn advance can weaken your own bishop while improving the opposing knight.

- ## Build and Use a Dominating Knight
	- **Karpov–Seirawan, Mar del Plata, 1982 (Diagram 60)**: Black's knight on c4 blocks White's doubled c-pawns and controls entry or escape squares deep in White's position. White's bishop on d4 is restricted by its own c3- and e5-pawns and tied to defense.
	- The advantage comes from the knight's concrete jobs, not merely its advanced location. Seirawan nevertheless drew after missing a winning continuation: a superior piece still needs accurate follow-through.
	- **Seirawan–Vukić, Niš, 1979**: `11.Bxd4! cxd4` trades a bishop for a knight while leaving Black's g7-bishop obstructed by its own d4-pawn. White follows with `12.Nb5` and `13.a4!` to secure the queenside position, then gains kingside space with `f4–f5`.
	- **Action**: assess exchanges by the pawn structure and pieces left afterward. Be willing to give up a bishop if the resulting position favors your remaining knight.

- ## Color Control and Converting the Advantage
	- A bishop stays on one square color. Against it, pawns on the opposite color can be safe from direct bishop attacks; even an active-looking bishop may lack targets.
	- A knight can maneuver to attack or defend squares of either color over successive moves. In the page 101 discussion, Black plans to bring the king to c5 to take over control of c4, freeing the knight for other work.
	- Do not preserve a superior minor piece at all costs: page 96 explains an exchange into a winning king-and-pawn ending because of Black's more active king and mobile pawn majority.
	- **Before choosing a move or trade**: identify useful diagonals, secure outposts, accessible targets, and the resulting pawn structure; then check king activity and concrete tactics.
