- **Source**: *Winning Chess Strategies* by Yasser Seirawan (with Jeremy Silman) — Chapter Four, “Understanding Where the Pieces Go,” the Queen section (pp. 82–85, Diagrams 50–52); pages photographed 2026-10-05. The final example continues beyond the supplied pages.
- **One-liner**: Give the queen useful work on a safe square; advance or centralize her when the opponent cannot profitably chase her, or when concrete tactics justify the risk.

- ## Develop the Team Before the Queen
	- The queen's range makes her powerful, but her value makes attacks by cheaper pieces costly: she often has to retreat while the opponent develops.
	- A **tempo** is a move's worth of time. Developing a knight while attacking the queen gains time because the opponent must spend a move responding.
	- Default plan: develop knights and bishops, place the queen on a safe and useful square within your territory, then activate the rooks on open or half-open files. Moving the queen off the back rank helps connect the rooks once the other pieces are clear.
	- Example: `1.e4 e5 2.Bc4 Bc5 3.Qh5` threatens `Qxe5+` and `Qxf7#`, but `3...Qe7` covers both. Black can then develop with `...Nf6`, attacking the queen. An early threat is less useful if the reply improves the opponent's position.

- ## Centralize When the Queen Can Stay
	- “Don't bring the queen out early” is a guideline. The key question is whether the opponent can attack her **while improving their own position**.
	- In the Scotch example, `1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.d4 exd4 4.Nxd4 Nxd4 5.Qxd4`, White's queen occupies the center, and Black lacks an immediate developing move that chases her away.
	- Black can attack with `5...c5`, but after a retreat such as `6.Qa4`, the pawn obstructs the f8-bishop's diagonal and leaves d5 weak. Chasing a queen is not automatically a gain if it damages your position.

- ## A Modest Square Can Do Valuable Work
	- When advanced squares are unsafe, choose a square that supports pawns, pressures a weakness, or clears a rook's path.
	- In Diagram 51, White's `Qc2` reinforces e4; `Qd2` is another option, pressuring d6. Black's `...Qe7` supports the weak d6-pawn.
	- The continuation `1.Qc2 Qe7 2.Rad1 Rad8 3.Rd2` shows the queen enabling the rest of the army: White prepares to double rooks on the d-file while Black defends d6.

- ## Accept Exposure Only After Calculating
	- In Diagram 52 (Larsen–Fischer, Candidates Match, 1971), White's `b4` attacks Black's queen on a5. Fischer chooses the active `...Qe5!` instead of retreating to d8.
	- On e5, the queen cooperates with the g7-bishop along the h8–a1 diagonal and attacks e4, but is also vulnerable to attack with few hiding places.
	- The book supplies concrete answers to apparent threats: `Bf4` or `Bd4` permits `...Nxe4!`, winning a pawn; `Bd3` permits `...Ng4!`, creating threats against h2 and c3. Activity is justified by these tactical resources, not by the queen's central appearance alone.

- ## Before Moving the Queen
	- **Purpose**: What pawn, piece, square, or mating threat will she support?
	- **Harassment**: Can a pawn, knight, or bishop attack her while developing or gaining useful space?
	- **Response**: If attacked, where can she retreat—or what forcing tactic makes retreat unnecessary?
	- **Teamwork**: Does this move help the other pieces, or spend another turn moving the queen while they remain undeveloped?
