- **One-liner**: Products sold as soy sauce can be fermented, made from hydrolyzed vegetable protein, or blended; identify the production method from product-specific evidence rather than the name or packaging alone.
- **Related**: [[Food Science]] · [[Fermentation]] · [[Food Labeling]]

- ## Three production routes

	- | Route | How it works | What to look for |
	  | --- | --- | --- |
	  | Brewed / fermented | Koji culture acts on soybeans and often wheat; the mash ferments and ages in brine before pressing and finishing. | A documented brewing process; “naturally brewed,” “traditionally brewed,” or honjozo terminology. |
	  | Non-brewed hydrolyzed-protein seasoning | Vegetable proteins are broken down by acid or enzymes; the hydrolysate becomes the seasoning base. | Hydrolyzed soy, corn, wheat, or vegetable protein in the ingredients. |
	  | Mixed / blended | Hydrolyzed protein is combined with a fermented component, before further fermentation or after brewing. | Both fermented soy sauce and hydrolyzed protein, or an explicit mixed-method description. |

	- The distinction is **how the flavor is produced**. Protein breakdown occurs in fermentation too; the word “hydrolysis” by itself does not mean an acid-manufactured product.
	- Japan's **JAS 1703:2021**, sections 3.2–3.8, distinguishes honjozo, mixed fermenting, and mixing methods. Its definition of soy sauce includes mixtures, and its koji definition permits defatted soybeans. Neither Japanese origin nor defatted soybeans alone settles the production method. [JAS: Soy sauce (Shoyu), revised January 25, 2021](https://www.famic.go.jp/english/jas/_doc/jas1703.pdf).

- ## Why acid hydrolysis is faster
	- In the acid route, heated hydrochloric acid breaks vegetable proteins into amino-acid constituents. Neutralization and filtration follow; a Michigan regulator's inspection documents these operations at an HVP manufacturer using corn, soy, or wheat gluten. This describes an industrial process, not a verified Kari-Out factory recipe. [Michigan DEQ, Sensient Flavors inspection report, August 2015](https://www.egle.state.mi.us/aps/downloads/srn/B1633/B1633_SAR_20150806.pdf).
	- Kikkoman's educational material hosted by the Culinary Institute of America describes **15–20 hours of acid treatment and a few days for the non-brewed process**, versus approximately **six months** for its brewed sauce. Caramel color, corn syrup, and salt are described as typical additions to the hydrolyzed base. These timings and additions are examples, not specifications for every brand. [CIA ProChef / Kikkoman, “The Story of Soy Sauce”](https://www.ciaprochef.com/kikkoman/story/).
	- Brewing develops aroma, color, and flavor over time. Kikkoman describes its own fermentation as lasting several months; its claims about superior taste are producer marketing, so they should not be treated as an independent ranking of every sauce. [Kikkoman, “6 Soy Sauce Myths, Busted”](https://kikkomanusa.com/blog/6-soy-sauce-myths-busted/).

	- This supports describing that product as an **HVP-based seasoning**. The sheet does not provide its hydrolysis method, factory processing time, or evidence for every Kari-Out SKU.
	- **Corn syrup is absent from this ingredient list.** A generic account of non-brewed sauce manufacture should not be turned into a claim that this packet contains it.
	- Kari-Out currently lists its contact address in **Tarrytown, New York**. The passage's New Jersey description is not a reliable statement of its current headquarters; no current market-share evidence was established for “biggest maker.” [Kari-Out official website](https://kariout.com/).

- ## What happened at Codex
	- Japan sought production-specific soy-sauce labels; the USDA-led U.S. delegation and hydrolyzed-protein industry resisted mandatory distinctions. [Washington Post, 2004](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/business/2004/09/21/feud-ferments-between-soy-sauce-makers/e487892f-5790-497c-af35-5d52f5d6114b/).
	- The official **June 2005 background paper** records work beginning in **1998**. Its annex reproduces U.S. and International Hydrolyzed Protein Council requests to discontinue the standard, citing divergent national definitions, lack of consensus, and their view that a new commodity standard was unnecessary. [Codex ALINORM 05/28/8-Add.1, especially Annex III, printed pp. 11–12](https://www.fao.org/fao-who-codexalimentarius/sh-proxy/en/?lnk=1&url=https%253A%252F%252Fworkspace.fao.org%252Fsites%252Fcodex%252FShared%2BDocuments%252FArchive%252FMeetings%252FCAC%252Fcac28%252Fal2808Ae.pdf).
	- **July 2005 outcome: work was discontinued because consensus was absent.** The Commission left open reconsideration in the future. This was abandonment of a proposed international standard, not adoption of a new universal rule declaring all chemical versions equivalent to brewed sauce. [Codex 28th Session report, paragraphs 103–104](https://www.fao.org/4/a0214e/A0214E06.htm).

- ## A better way to read the label
	- **Look for positive evidence of brewing** in the label and the manufacturer's process description. The exact English phrase “naturally brewed” is a useful cue, but the sources above do not establish its absence as proof of non-fermentation.
	- **Read the complete ingredients.** Hydrolyzed protein identifies an added hydrolysate; it does not establish that the whole product is unfermented, because blended methods exist.
	- **Check the specific product.** A packet or grocery bottle can contain different types of sauce; packaging format and country of origin do not determine the process. The passage supplies no market data supporting a claim about the share of grocery bottles using HVP.
	- **Use precise language:** “not traditionally brewed” is the useful process distinction for an HVP-only seasoning. “Not soy sauce” adds a disputed naming judgment that varies with the standard and market.
