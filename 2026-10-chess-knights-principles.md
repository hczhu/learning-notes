- **Source**: *Winning Chess Strategies* by Yasser Seirawan (with Jeremy Silman) — Chapter Four, "Understanding Where the Pieces Go," the Knight section (pp. 45–51, Diagrams 18–25); pages photographed 2026-10-05.
- **One-liner**: The Knight is a **short-range** piece, so its entire strategic life is about getting **advanced** and **staying there** — which means it needs a **support point**: a square an enemy pawn cannot (or dare not) attack. Rank by rank its value climbs from "purely defensive" on the 1st–2nd to "superior to a Bishop" on the 5th.
- ## 1. Why amateurs distrust Knights
	- Seirawan's framing: the Knight plays second billing to the Bishop for many (**"the great Fischer loves Bishops"**), and is *"looked down on by many amateurs and secretly feared by others."*
	- The love–hate relationship explained: **a Bishop will out-perform a Knight in the majority of positions** — which breeds disdain for the horse — **but a Knight can wipe a Bishop off the face of the board if the correct environment appears.**
	- The two reasons amateurs distrust them, as listed in the book:
		- Their pieces and pawns **are always being forked** by the horrible beasts.
		- **They don't know how to use them properly.**
	- So the whole section is about reversing the second one: *"It's time to learn how to make the humble Knight rule the board!"*
- ## 2. The central concept: the support point
	- **Why it is needed**: unlike the long-range Bishop, the Knight is a **short-range piece**, which means **it must advance up the board to be strong**. But an advanced piece is easily attacked by enemy pieces and pawns.
	- So the Knight needs **a quiet square where it can safely rest while simultaneously casting a menacing shadow over the board**.
	- **Definition** — a square is a support point only if:
		- it **cannot be attacked by an enemy pawn**, *or*
		- **attacking it with a pawn would severely weaken the enemy's position**.
	- **Rule 1: *Knights need advanced support points to be effective.***
	- **You usually have to build one.** Occasionally a support point appears by enemy blunder, and then you *"rush your Knight over and gleefully attach it to its post."* Normally the opponent is not so generous, so **whole strategies are built around creating a support point** — an advanced Knight on a juicy one can be the first major step toward a resounding victory.
	- The counter-example to internalize: in Diagram 20, **g5 is not a support point** for the White Knight, because Black kicks it away easily with **...h7–h6**. A square is only a post if the pawn that would attack it can't, or mustn't, come.
- ## 3. The rank ladder — a Knight's value by how far it has advanced
	- **The ladder at a glance:**
	  ```
	    rank 6 |  "a bone lodged in someone's throat" - a parasite deep in
	           |  enemy territory, attacking men and controlling key squares
	    -------+-------------------------------------------------------------
	    rank 5 |  SUPERIOR to a Bishop (when on a support point)
	    -------+-------------------------------------------------------------
	    rank 4 |  generally AS GOOD AS a Bishop; well poised for attack
	           |  AND defense
	    -------+-------------------------------------------------------------
	    rank 3 |  useful for defense, but one hop from the 5th rank
	    -------+-------------------------------------------------------------
	    rank 2 |  purely defensive
	    rank 1 |  "will never be effective attackers"
	  ```
	- | Rank | Verdict | The book's evidence |
	  |---|---|---|
	  | **1st & 2nd** | **Purely defensive.** *"Your Knights will not be world-beaters if you allow them to lounge around on the 1st and 2nd ranks!"* | Diagram 18: materially equal but bad for Black — White's Knight on the 5th controls **eight** squares, Black's on h8 controls a **pathetic two**. Diagram 19: a 1st-rank Knight claims **4** squares and a 2nd-rank one **6** — the two together (**10**) barely beat a single White Knight on c3 (**8**), and they need several moves just to reach enemy territory |
	  | **3rd** | Useful for defense, **but one move from the 5th** | Diagram 20: ...**Nf6** brings the Knight from the horrible h8 to the superior f6, where it defends h7 *and* aspires to d5, e4, or g4 |
	  | **4th** | **Generally as good as a Bishop**; poised for attack and defense | Diagram 21: the Knight has a firm support point on d5 — **defended by the e6-pawn and un-chaseable by any enemy pawn**. Its range: defends b6, attacks b4, can pounce on c3/e3/f4, can retreat to c7/e7/f6 |
	  | **5th** | **Superior to a Bishop** (on a support point) | It *"eyes many points deep in the enemy camp, threatens the enemy pawns, and helps the rest of its own army in forming an attack."* Diagram 22: Black has **two** fine support points (e4 joins an attack on c3; g4 works with the Queen against h2), while White's Knight has **none** — c5 is met by ...b6 |
	  | **6th** | **A bone in the throat** — a virtual parasite | Diagram 23: the Knight on e3 eyes g2 and c4 and **denies f1, d1, and c2 to the White Rooks** — c1–c2 to defend a2 becomes suicidal |
	- **The transferable judgment**: counting the squares a Knight controls is a fast, concrete way to evaluate it. Two for a cornered Knight versus eight for a centralized one is not a nuance — it is the difference between having a piece and not.
- ## 4. Closed positions belong to the Knight
	- **Why**: in positions with locked pawns (**closed positions**), the other pieces are blocked by the pawns — but **Knights simply jump over them. The pawns don't interfere with their activity at all.**
	- Diagram 24: the White Bishop suffers badly on clogged diagonals, while the Black Knight calmly **routes itself to its post** — ...Nf8, ...Ng6, ...Nf4 — reaching the f4 support point by hopping over everything in the way.
	- **The practical skill this implies**: a Knight's journey is usually **a multi-move regrouping**, not a single leap. Seeing a good square three moves away and walking the Knight to it is the characteristic Knight technique.
- ## 5. The Knight as blockader of a passed pawn
	- **Knights are "known as the very finest blockers (called blockaders) of passed pawns."** This guard duty cripples most pieces; the Knight thrives at it.
	- Diagram 25 — White has the Bishop, more central space, and a **powerful passed pawn on d5**, yet **the position is better for Black**, because White's Bishop is inactive and Black's Knight is headed for d6 via **...Ne8 and ...Nd6**.
	- On d6, that one Knight does **five jobs at once**: attacks c4 **and** e4 (tying White down to their defense), keeps White's pieces off b5, eyes b7/c8/e8/f7, and **stops the passed pawn dead in its tracks**.
	- **Why a Knight and not another piece on that square** — the comparison is the real lesson:
	- | Piece on d6 | Why it fails there |
	  |---|---|
	  | **Bishop** | Looks *"more like a tall pawn than a high-ranking piece"* — Black's own pawns on e5 and c5 block it |
	  | **Rook** | Terrible — **a Rook needs open files to roam** |
	  | **Queen** | Far too important *"to play nursemaid to a mere passed pawn"* |
	  | **Knight** | Happy — because it can **perform an offensive and a defensive role at the same time**, which the others simply can't |
	- The root cause is the same one that makes Knights good in closed positions: **the ability to jump over other men** means its own blockading duty never costs it its activity.
- ## 6. The illustrative game
	- **Petrosian–Bondarevsky, Moscow 1950** — *Dutch Defense*: **1.Nf3 e6 2.g3 f5 3.Bg2 Nf6 4.0-0 Be7 5.d4 0-0 6.c4 c6 7.Qc2 Qe8 8.Nbd2 d5**.
	- The theme: **Black willingly weakens an important square**, hoping White won't be able to exploit it — and White *"clamps down on this weak point in masterful fashion."* Black gains space and control of e5, but **a hole now exists on e5**, and Black's grip on e4 is more tenuous than White's grip on e5.
	- This is §2 played out over a whole game: the support point was not found, it was **manufactured out of the opponent's pawn structure**.
- ## 7. Takeaways
	- **A Knight's strength is almost entirely a function of where it stands.** The same piece is worth two squares on h8 and eight on the 5th rank. Unlike the Bishop, it has no long-range compensation for being badly placed.
	- **Therefore: find the square first, route the Knight second.** The support point is the object of the plan; the Knight's path to it is just execution.
	- **A square is a support point only if the enemy pawn that would evict it can't come, or mustn't.** Checking that one condition is most of the evaluation.
	- **Knight vs. Bishop is an environment question, not a value question.** Open and fluid favors the Bishop; locked pawns, a secure advanced outpost, or blockading duty in front of a passed pawn favor the Knight. (See the companion note on [[2026-10-chess-bishops-principles]], which argues the same point from the other side.)
	- **The Knight is the only piece that attacks and defends at full strength simultaneously**, because nothing blocks it. That single property explains its dominance in closed positions *and* as a blockader.
