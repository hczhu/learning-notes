- **Source**: *Winning Chess Strategies* by Yasser Seirawan (with Jeremy Silman) — Chapter Ten, “Faulty Strategies,” pp. 194–207, from the supplied photographs.
- **One-liner**: Let the actual position—not habit, wishful thinking, or emotion—determine your plan, and check that tactics make it work.

- ## Principles

	- **Make a plan, but keep reassessing it.** Aimless moves waste opportunities; stubbornly following an outdated plan does too. Notice changes and be ready to exploit the opponent's mistakes.
	- **An advantage matters only if you can use it.** A bishop needs useful diagonals; a pawn majority needs a realistic route to a passed pawn. Compare what you gain with what you concede, including enemy activity and king threats.
	- **Attack where the position supports you.** A queenside or space advantage does not automatically justify a kingside attack. Before a flank pawn storm, check your king's safety and whether the opponent can break open the center. Against a wing attack, look for central counterplay.
	- **Develop with awareness, not on autopilot.** Routine development is no substitute for recognizing danger. A closed center can open through a pawn break or sacrifice; if you fall behind or misjudge the position, switch to concrete defense.
	- **Treat familiar positions as new positions.** Small differences in pawn placement, diagonals, or king location can make a standard move wrong. Use remembered patterns as candidates, not conclusions.
	- **Tactics must validate strategy.** A positionally attractive move can fail to checks, captures, or threats. Look for loose pieces, pins, and lines that can open after exchanges—even a distant rook behind several blockers can become a target.
	- **Play the board, not the opponent's reputation.** Fear encourages unnecessary retreat; overconfidence invites unsound attacks and overlooked counterplay. If emotion takes over, pause, breathe, and reassess objectively within your available clock time.

- ## Before Moving

	- What feature of this position makes my plan appropriate?
	- What changed after the last move, and what is my opponent threatening?
	- What is their strongest forcing reply, especially a central break?
	- Am I choosing this move because it works—or because it is familiar, aggressive, or reassuring?
