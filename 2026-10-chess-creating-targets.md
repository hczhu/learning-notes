- **Source**: *Winning Chess Strategies* by Yasser Seirawan (with Jeremy Silman) — **Chapter Seven, "The Creation of Targets"** (pp. 137–153, Diagrams 84–91, Tests 19); pages photographed 2026-10-05/06.
- **One-liner**: Checkmate is almost never the first plan — it is the last step after a **positional** struggle that manufactures weaknesses. The chapter's whole content is one mindset plus one correction: **target consciousness**, and the realization that **nothing is weak because of its name — a pawn is weak only if it can be *attacked*, and a square is weak only if you can *occupy and use* it.** Targets do not appear on their own; **you create them.**
- ## 1. Target consciousness — the mindset
	- *"Most people think that chess is all about checkmating the enemy King. Though this is technically true, in the vast majority of master games, an actual checkmate occurs only after the waging of a bitter positional struggle."*
	- The actual winning sequence: build **a series of small advantages** → these **inevitably reveal weaknesses** in the enemy camp → **penetrate onto weak squares** and **win weak pawns** → win *"by virtue of the superior size of his army."*
	- **Target consciousness** = always looking for ways to exploit the opponent's weaknesses; becoming **obsessed with creating targets (weak points)**; once a target materializes, **systematically searching for ways to hit it**, and **not resting until every weakness of the opponent has been turned into an advantage of your own.**
	- > ***Emulate the great chess masters. They are all mini-Terminators. They scope out targets and hunt them down mercilessly until they can use them to their own advantage!***
- ## 2. The central correction: labels are not weaknesses
	- **This is the idea the whole chapter turns on.** Calling a pawn *isolated*, *doubled*, or *backward* is a **label**; calling it weak is a **claim about attackability**. Seirawan's word for conflating the two is blunt: ***"a form of chess bigotry."***
		- ***A pawn is weak only if it can be attacked.*** *"If an enemy piece cannot get to it, then there is no reason to worry about its safety, regardless of whether it is isolated, doubled, or backward."*
	- The same test, restated for squares — and the student's objection is the natural one: *"You always say a pawn is weak only if it can be attacked. How can a square be weak if you can never capture a square?"*
		- The answer: **you *can* capture a square — "by capturing a square, I mean you can make it serve your army."** You own a square when it is **well covered by your own pawns and nailed down by your own pieces** (a Knight's *support point* from [[2026-10-chess-knights-principles]] is exactly this).
	  ```
	    TWO kinds of target, one test:
	  
	      a weak PAWN    -> something you can ATTACK
	      a weak SQUARE  -> something you can OCCUPY and USE
	  
	    Neither is decided by its NAME. Both are decided by
	    whether your pieces can actually get there.
	  ```
- ## 3. Weak pawns — the same defect, twice, with opposite verdicts
	- The chapter proves the point by putting two positions side by side.
	- | | **Diagram 84** (Petrosian–Barcza 1955) | **Diagram 85** (a children's-opening position) |
	  |---|---|---|
	  | **Structure** | Black: isolated b6 **on an open file**, isolated d- and h-pawns, doubled f-pawns, **four pawn islands** to White's one | White takes doubled e-pawns voluntarily, via **7.Be3!** and `7...Bxe3 8.fxe3` |
	  | **Verdict** | *"Black's pawns are all horribly weak… so rotten that White will just eat one after another"* — they *"drop like overripe apples"* | *"Clearly, doubled pawns are not necessarily weak!"* |
	  | **Why** | Every weak pawn is **reachable**: open files for the rooks, no pawn able to defend them | **e4 is well defended; e3 is not on an open file**, so enemy rooks can't touch it, and the one piece that annoys it (a knight on g4) is chased off by `h3` |
	  | **Upside** | — | The f2-pawn *"was doing nothing at all"*; on e3 it becomes **a dynamic contributor**, with e3+e4 covering **d4, d5, f4 and f5**, and the vacated f-file giving the rooks a **half-open file** |
	- **The generalizable test** before you call anything weak, or accept a structural "defect":
	  ```
	    "isolated / doubled / backward"  <- that is only a LABEL
	  
	    Ask instead:
	      1. Can an enemy piece actually REACH a square that hits it?
	      2. Is it on an open or half-open file?
	      3. Can any pawn of mine ever defend it?
	  
	    yes, yes, no   =  a real target
	    anything else  =  a label, not a weakness
	  ```
	- Note the two cases the chapter gives where **accepting doubled pawns is good**: `7...Bb6` inviting `8.Bxb6? axb6!` (which hands Black **a half-open a-file** and doubled b-pawns *"that are not weak at all"*), and `7...Bg4! 8.Bxc5 dxc5`, where the c-pawns are safe after `...b6`, **c5 helps control d4**, and vacating d6 opens **the half-open d-file** for queen and rooks.
		- **The pattern**: a doubled pawn trades *mobility* for *coverage of key squares* **plus a half-open file**. If the pawns can't be attacked, that is a straight profit.
- ## 4. Creating weak pawns — the minority attack
	- *"Attackable weak pawns might result from an enemy error, but you cannot count on any opponent to make this kind of error. It is up to you to find ways to create these weaknesses."*
	- > ***Employ two pawns to attack a pawn majority of three, with the goal of leaving your enemy with weak squares and pawns.***
	  ```
	    MINORITY ATTACK -- your 2 pawns attack their 3
	  
	      you:    a2  b2   --  push b4-b5, then bxc6
	      them:   a7  b7  c6
	  
	    After bxc6 the defender must recapture, and BOTH recaptures hurt:
	      ...bxc6  ->  backward c-pawn on an OPEN FILE
	      ...dxc6  ->  weak d5 instead
	  
	    Either way: a pawn no pawn of theirs can ever defend. Permanent.
	  ```
	- **Why it points where it does** (Reshevsky–Miagmasuren 1967): White plays on the queenside *"because his pawns point in that direction"* (the f2–e3–d4 chain) and give him extra territory there. The defender's counterpart plan is **counterplay on the kingside, where *his* extra territory is**.
	- **The follow-through, which is the actually transferable part** (Averbakh–Donner 1962, a complete game): the position is quiet with **no weaknesses on either side**, so White **manufactures one**:
		- **12.Bxf6** — removes the defender of b4 so the pawn can advance safely. *(Deflect the piece that guards the square you need.)*
		- **13.b4 … 14.b5 … 15.bxc6 bxc6** — *"The game now revolves around the newly created weakness on c6."*
		- **16.Rac1, 17.Rc2, 19.Rfc1** — pile everything onto the new target.
		- **20.Nbd2** — bring the knight back into play (*"never leave a Knight sitting around on the 1st rank"*) **and** deny c4 to the enemy horse.
		- **21.Rc3, 22.Kg2** — restrict the enemy bishop, give the king luft, add a defender; the attacker tidies up **because the defender has no time**.
		- **23.e4!, 25.e5** — once the opponent is tied down, **take space on the other side**. (He doesn't fear `23...dxe4 24.Nxe4` giving him an isolated d-pawn, **because no Black piece can reach it** — §2 again.)
		- **29.Nb3, 30.Nbd4** — *"always strive to dominate the square directly in front of an isolated pawn!"*
	- > ***Weak enemy pawns don't just magically appear; you must create them.***
- ## 5. Weak squares — how you manufacture a hole
	- **Karpov–Browne, San Antonio 1972.** After `1.e4 c5 2.b3 Nf6 3.Bb2 g6`, *"a glance shows that no weak squares exist."* Karpov changes that with **4.Bxf6! exf6**.
		- **The reasoning is the whole lesson**: he does **not** give up his strong fianchettoed bishop to double the pawns — *"he sees that the doubled pawns won't be weak in any way."* He gives it up because **dragging the e-pawn off e7 means it can no longer control d5.** *"In other words, White is playing to create a weak square!"*
		- Then the occupation: **5.Nc3** trains the first piece on it, **6.g3 and 7.Bg2** add the light-squared bishop *"to join in the d5 orgy,"* and **9.Nge2** heads for f4 to add more. *"Once he creates the weak square, he immediately brings all of his pieces to bear on it."*
	- **The asymmetry worth memorizing** — Black also has a knight, a bishop and a pawn hitting **d4**. Does he own it? **No.**
	  ```
	    WHY WHITE OWNS d5 BUT BLACK DOES NOT OWN d4
	  
	      d5:  Black's e-pawn was deflected (4.Bxf6 exf6)
	           -> NO black pawn can ever defend d5        -> a real hole
	  
	      d4:  White simply plays 8.e3
	           -> a pawn now covers d4                    -> not a hole
	  
	    A square guarded by a PAWN is not weak, no matter how many
	    enemy pieces aim at it. Pieces visiting != owning.
	  ```
	- **Fischer–Durao, Havana 1966** is the same method: the position *"seems solid for both sides,"* but Fischer plays **1.dxc5 dxc5** *"because he wants to get Black's d-pawn away from d6 and lay claim to both d6 and f6"* — then immediately points everything at them (Bf4 or Bg5, Nbd2-e4, Rd1).
	- **The general recipe, in both games**: find the enemy pawn that is the *only* possible defender of the square you want, **force it to move or trade it off**, then bring every piece to the vacancy.
- ## 6. The limits — a square is not a plan
	- > ***Control of a square alone will not necessarily lead to victory. You must also have other advantages that combine to bring the opponent down.***
	- **Karpov–Mecking, Hastings 1971–72** (Diagram 89) is the illustration. White owns d5 and his bishop there is magnificent. Black's bishop *can* reach d4 — and it is still worthless, because **it can't work with the rest of his army**: White's rooks and pawns are attacking the kingside and the bishop aims that way too, while *"Black's Bishop will look very picturesque on d4, but it simply can't do anything in relation to the other Black pieces."*
	- > ***Chess is a team effort. One macho piece cannot win a game by itself!***
	- > ***A strong piece + a captured square(s) = trouble for your opponent!***
	- **The line-opening rule from the same game**: White declines `2.h5??` because *"that move totally locks the position up on the side of the board where White wants to play."*
		- > ***Open lines when you want to attack, and close lines when you are trying to defend.***
- ## 7. Fighting over a square — prevention as a plan
	- Diagram 90 is the two-sided case: White's pawns (d4, e5) point kingside, Black's point queenside, and *"you should always attack with pawns in positions with closed centers."* The naive handling is a pure race — but Seirawan asks a better question.
	- **The habit to build: ask what each side can and cannot prevent.** Here White **cannot** stop `...c6-c5`, but Black **can** stop `f4-f5` — *"Because the f4-f5 push is so important to White, isn't it worth Black spending a couple of moves to prevent it?"*
	- **And prevent it profitably.** `1...f5?` prevents it but leaves **a backward e-pawn on an open file** — trading one target for another. The right move is **1...Nh6!**, which stops `f4-f5` **and** announces `...Nf5` as a permanent post; then **3...h5!** stops `g2-g4` so White can never reclaim f5.
	- **The payoff is structural**: White's bishop and rook stay *"doomed to remain trapped behind the f4-pawn,"* Black's knight has a magnificent post — and Black then **resumes his own plan** (`...c5`, `...Nc6`, `...Qb6`, all striking d4). *"The fact that Black first stopped White's plan makes this whole strategy all the sweeter."*
	- **Karpov–Spassky, Leningrad 1974** adds the two refinements:
		- **Permanent vs. temporary holes.** Black's `10...a5` stops the a-pawn but concedes **b5**, and White's `11.a4` concedes **b4** — *"The difference is that the b4-square is not a permanent hole,"* because White can later play `c2-c3` and reclaim it. **Count whether a pawn can ever come back to cover the square; that is what separates a hole from an inconvenience.**
		- **Trade to keep a square.** **20.Bxd7** gives up the two bishops on purpose, because that knight *"could eventually play to b6 and fight for c4."* > ***When playing for possession of a square, don't hesitate to trade pieces that can challenge you for that post.***
- ## 8. Takeaways
	- **Weakness is a function of reachability, not vocabulary.** Isolated, doubled, backward — all labels. The question is whether an enemy piece can get to it, whether it sits on an open file, and whether a pawn can ever defend it.
	- **The two target types are one idea.** A weak pawn is something you can **attack**; a weak square is something you can **occupy and use**. Both are "making the enemy position serve you."
	- **A square guarded by a pawn is not a hole.** Pieces aiming at a square is not ownership — Black had three attackers on d4 and still didn't own it, because `e3` existed. The corollary is the method: **to create a hole, deflect or trade the one pawn that could defend it.**
	- **Targets are manufactured, not found.** `4.Bxf6!`, `1.dxc5`, `12.Bxf6` and the whole minority attack are the same move: **force a structural concession, then move in.** Quiet positions with no weaknesses are an invitation to create one.
	- **Create, then converge.** Every example follows the same two-beat rhythm — one move that makes the target, then *every* piece pointed at it. Half-measures leave you with a weakness you can't actually reach.
	- **Prevention is a plan.** Ask what each side can and cannot stop, and spend tempi stopping the opponent's key push — but **only in a way that doesn't create a new target of your own** (`1...Nh6!`, not `1...f5?`).
	- **One great piece is not a position.** Control of a square only wins when it combines with other advantages, and a beautifully posted piece that can't coordinate with its army is decoration.
